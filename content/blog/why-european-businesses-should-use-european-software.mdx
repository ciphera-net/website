In October 2025, the International Criminal Court's prosecutor was locked out of his Microsoft account after US sanctions hit. Within weeks, the entire court — 1,800 workstations — switched to European open-source software ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/31/international_criminal_court_ditches_office/), Oct 2025). It wasn't a gradual transition. It was forced.

That story captures something broader. US companies account for 83% of all GDPR fines — EUR 4.68 billion out of EUR 5.65 billion total ([Center for Data Innovation](https://datainnovation.org/2025/04/europes-gdpr-fines-against-us-firms-are-unfair-and-disproportionate), Apr 2025). The question isn't whether European alternatives exist. It's why so many businesses still haven't switched.

## The Legal Problem You Can't Outsource

Meta was fined EUR 1.2 billion for transferring EU data to the US — the largest GDPR fine ever issued ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/news/2023/12-billion-euro-fine-facebook-result-edpb-binding-decision_en), May 2023). That single penalty illustrates a conflict between US and EU law that no contract or data processing agreement can resolve.

The problem starts with the CLOUD Act. Passed in 2018, it gives US law enforcement the power to compel any US-headquartered company to produce data — regardless of where that data is stored. A Microsoft server in Frankfurt? Still subject to US jurisdiction. An AWS instance in Stockholm? Same thing.

This directly conflicts with GDPR Article 48, which prohibits transferring personal data to a third country based solely on a foreign court order. Comply with the CLOUD Act, and you breach GDPR. Refuse, and you face US legal penalties. There's no clean exit.

The enforcement record makes the risk concrete. Uber was fined EUR 290 million for transferring European drivers' sensitive data to the US ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/news/2024/dutch-sa-imposes-fine-290-million-euro-uber-because-transfers-drivers-data-us_en), Jul 2024). The European Data Protection Supervisor ruled that the European Commission's own use of Microsoft 365 violated EU data protection law ([EDPS](https://www.edps.europa.eu/press-publications/press-news/press-releases/2024/european-commissions-use-microsoft-365-infringes-data-protection-law-eu-institutions-and-bodies_en), Mar 2024). Even the EU's own institutions couldn't make American software compliant.

Microsoft France's general manager testified under oath that he "cannot guarantee data safety from access by US authorities" — even for data hosted in Europe. If Microsoft can't guarantee it, who can?

## What Are European Governments Actually Doing?

France is migrating 200,000 civil servants from Microsoft Teams and Zoom to Visio, a sovereign open-source platform, with full deployment by 2027 ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2026/01/27/france-to-ditch-us-platforms-microsoft-teams-zoom-for-sovereign-platform-amid-security-con), Jan 2026). This isn't an experiment. It's national policy — and France isn't alone.

Germany's Schleswig-Holstein is moving 30,000 PCs to LibreOffice and Linux. As of 2024, 80% had already transitioned, saving an estimated EUR 15 million per year ([LibreOffice Blog](https://blog.documentfoundation.org/blog/2024/04/04/german-state-moving-30000-pcs-to-libreoffice/), Apr 2024). Denmark is dropping Microsoft Office, citing sovereignty concerns and US political tensions.

<BlogImage src="/blog/images/pexels-3184339.jpg" alt="European parliament building exterior with digital shield icons overlaid, symbolising government-led digital sovereignty initiatives" />

The ICC replaced Microsoft with OpenDesk across 1,800 workstations after US sanctions locked out the prosecutor ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/31/international_criminal_court_ditches_office/), Oct 2025). The EU Commission launched a EUR 180 million sovereign cloud tender for all EU institutions ([European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/commission-moves-forward-cloud-sovereignty-eur-180-million-tender-2025-10-10_en), Oct 2025). And in November 2025, all 27 EU member states signed the Berlin Declaration for European Digital Sovereignty ([Council of the EU](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-15781-2025-INIT/en/pdf)).

These aren't pilot programmes. They're coordinated policy shifts from the highest levels of European government. When a government bets its own infrastructure on European software, that's a signal worth paying attention to.

<BlogBlockquote>

**Our take:** Governments don't switch software stacks for fun. The administrative cost is enormous. When France commits to migrating 200,000 civil servants, it's because the legal and security risks of staying on American platforms now outweigh the pain of moving. Businesses should read this as a leading indicator, not an anomaly.

</BlogBlockquote>

## How Fast Is the Market Catching Up?

European sovereign cloud spending is projected to triple — from $6.9 billion in 2025 to $23.1 billion in 2027 ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-02-09-gartner-says-worldwide-sovereign-cloud-iaas-spending-will-total-us-dollars-80-billion-in-2026), Feb 2026). The money is moving. But the market share picture is still lopsided.

US hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, and Google — still hold roughly 70% of the EU cloud market. European providers sit at just 15% ([Synergy Research](https://www.srgresearch.com/articles/european-cloud-providers-local-market-share-now-holds-steady-at-15), 2025). That gap exists because inertia is powerful. Switching costs are real. And frankly, AWS and Azure are excellent products.

But the alternatives are maturing quickly. Nextcloud added over 2 million new professional users in 2025 alone. The French Ministry of Education deployed it for 400,000 employees ([Nextcloud](https://nextcloud.com/blog/press_releases/sovereign-workspace-momentum/), 2025). Office.eu launched in March 2026 as a 100% European alternative to Microsoft 365.

So why haven't more businesses switched? Honestly, it's the same reason people stay in bad contracts — the upfront cost of leaving feels worse than the ongoing risk of staying. But that calculus is changing fast as fines climb and regulations tighten.

## What Does a Practical European Stack Look Like in 2026?

European sovereign cloud spending tripling to $23.1 billion by 2027 means the ecosystem is expanding rapidly ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-02-09-gartner-says-worldwide-sovereign-cloud-iaas-spending-will-total-us-dollars-80-billion-in-2026), Feb 2026). Let's be honest about the trade-offs. Some European tools have fewer integrations than their US counterparts. Ecosystem effects are real — if your team lives in Google Workspace, switching everything at once is disruptive. And some categories, like CRM, still lack strong European contenders.

But the point isn't ideological purity. It's reducing legal exposure. You don't need to replace everything overnight. Start with the categories that carry the most privacy risk: analytics, file sharing, and email. Those three changes alone eliminate the majority of US data exposure.

## What's at Stake If You Don't Switch?

FISA Section 702 surveillance targets rose to 292,000 in 2024, and FBI queries increased 35% in 2025 ([ODNI](https://www.dni.gov/files/CLPT/documents/2025_ASTR_for_CY2024.pdf), 2025). Meanwhile, the NIS2 Directive means European executives now bear personal liability for cybersecurity failures — with fines up to EUR 10 million or 2% of global revenue.

US government data requests to Big Tech have surged dramatically over the past decade: 927% at Apple and 668% at Meta ([Proton](https://proton.me/blog/big-tech-data-requests-surge), 2025). These aren't targeted investigations. They're bulk operations at industrial scale.

The EU-US Data Privacy Framework — the legal mechanism that currently legitimises most EU-to-US data transfers — is already at the CJEU on appeal. If it falls, thousands of businesses will scramble to restructure their data flows overnight. We've seen this movie twice before with Safe Harbor and Privacy Shield. Both ended the same way.

<BlogBlockquote>

**Our take:** The risk isn't just regulatory fines. It's operational dependency on a foreign legal system you can't control. When a single US sanctions decision can lock an international court out of its own email, that's a business continuity risk that belongs on the board agenda — not buried in an IT compliance report.

</BlogBlockquote>
