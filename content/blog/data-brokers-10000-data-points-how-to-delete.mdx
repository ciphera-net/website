## What Do Data Brokers Actually Know About You?

Acxiom maintains profiles on [2.5 billion consumers](https://www.acxiom.com/customer-data/) with over 10,000 data attributes per person — making it the largest commercial data broker by coverage. Your advertising profile is worth roughly $263 per year to the industry; a complete financial profile sells for around $100 ([WebFX](https://www.webfx.com/blog/internet/what-are-data-brokers-and-what-is-your-data-worth-infographic/)).

They're not alone. Experian maintains 5,000 data points across 1.4 billion consumers, sliced into 2,400 audience segments. That includes credit behaviour, purchase history, lifestyle markers, and demographic predictions. The categories are broad: identity, financial, behavioural, health inferences, political affiliation, religion, and precise location.

Then there's Gravy Analytics, which collected 17 billion location signals per day from over 1 billion devices. The [FTC's December 2024 complaint](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/12/ftc-takes-action-against-gravy-analytics-venntel-unlawfully-selling-location-data-tracking-consumers) revealed they tracked people to medical clinics, military bases, and places of worship — then sold that data to whoever would pay.

Clearview AI scraped over 30 billion photos for a facial recognition database and was fined a combined EUR 65.2 million by data protection authorities across France, Italy, Austria, Greece, and the Netherlands ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/national-news/2022/french-sa-fines-clearview-ai-eur-20-million_en)). Has anyone who appeared in those 30 billion photos ever given consent? Of course not.

The EDPB published a Data Brokers Market Study in March 2026, identifying eight distinct broker types active in Europe — from people-search engines to real-time bidding data aggregators. The ecosystem is massive, opaque, and largely invisible to the people it profits from.

## What Happens When Data Brokers Get Breached?

Data broker breaches have cost consumers an estimated $20.8 billion in damages ([CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/economy/technology/2026/02/data-brokers-leak-personal-data-cost-americans-21-billion/), Feb 2026). When companies that hoard data on billions of people get hacked, the fallout is catastrophic — and it keeps happening.

**National Public Data (August 2024):** A breach exposed [2.9 billion records](https://www.ibm.com/think/news/national-public-data-breach-publishes-private-data-billions-us-citizens) including Social Security numbers, full names, and address histories. The dataset was posted on the dark web. The company filed for bankruptcy two months later — leaving affected consumers with no entity to hold accountable.

**Gravy Analytics (January 2025):** Attackers stole 17TB of data. A 1.4GB fragment alone contained over [30 million location records](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/13/gravy-analytics-data-broker-breach-trove-of-location-data-threatens-privacy-millions/) traced to 12,373 app brands including Tinder, Grindr, and Candy Crush. Here's the irony: the FTC had banned Gravy Analytics from selling sensitive location data in December 2024. They were breached weeks later.

**LexisNexis (May 2025):** Leaked GitHub credentials exposed 364,000 people's data. **TransUnion (2025):** 4 million people affected. Each incident follows the same pattern — companies that collect everything protect almost nothing.

Oracle shut down its $2 billion Data Cloud business in September 2024, citing privacy regulation. When one of the world's largest tech companies exits the data brokerage market, it tells you which way the wind is blowing.

**Our take:** The data broker business model is fundamentally broken. These companies collect data on billions of people, store it in centralised databases, and secure it with the same mediocre practices we see everywhere else. The breach isn't the exception — it's the inevitable outcome of hoarding data you don't need and can't protect.

## How Do You Delete Your Data Under GDPR?

GDPR Article 17 gives Europeans the right to erasure — any data broker processing your personal data must delete it within 30 days of a written request. Clearview AI's EUR 65.2 million in combined fines across five countries proves that European authorities actually enforce this ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/national-news/2022/french-sa-fines-clearview-ai-eur-20-million_en)). The maximum penalty is 4% of global annual turnover.

The process itself is straightforward in theory. Send a written request citing Article 17 to the company's Data Protection Officer. If they refuse, ignore you, or don't respond within 30 days, file a complaint with your national Data Protection Authority. The DPA investigates and can impose fines.

In practice? It's tedious. Each broker has its own opt-out process. A [CalMatters/Markup investigation](https://calmatters.org/economy/technology/2026/02/data-brokers-leak-personal-data-cost-americans-21-billion/) found that many brokers use no-index tags to hide their opt-out pages from search engines — deliberately making them harder to find. Worse, your data often reappears within 30 to 90 days as brokers re-acquire it from public records and data-sharing partners.

Automated deletion services have emerged to handle this at scale. Incogni covers 420+ brokers and is the only service with a Deloitte-audited verification (roughly $7/month). DeleteMe handles 85 to 262 brokers ($8/month). Optery covers 350+ ($15/month). None are perfect — but they're far better than trying to track down hundreds of opt-out forms manually.

California's Delete Act launched its DROP platform in January 2026, giving US residents one-click deletion. Europe doesn't have an equivalent centralised tool yet — but GDPR's individual enforcement rights are arguably stronger. The 750+ registered US data brokers are only the ones that comply with registration laws. Hundreds more don't ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/06/why-are-hundreds-data-brokers-not-registering-states), Jun 2025).

<BlogBlockquote>

**Our take:** Deletion requests are necessary, but they're a game of whack-a-mole. You delete your data from one broker, and it reappears 60 days later because another broker sold it back. The real solution isn't just deleting data after it's collected — it's preventing collection in the first place.

</BlogBlockquote>


## How Do You Stop Data Collection Before It Starts?

Prevention beats deletion. While automated services chase brokers you already know about, reducing your data footprint stops new collection at the source. The 73% of people who feel powerless over their data ([Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/10/18/how-americans-view-data-privacy/), 2023) aren't wrong about the problem — but they do have practical options.

**Limit app permissions aggressively.** Location, contacts, microphone — review what each app actually needs versus what it requests. Gravy Analytics built a 17-billion-signal-per-day empire largely from apps that didn't need location access but collected it anyway.

**Use privacy-focused email for signups.** Gmail and Outlook scan inbox content for ad targeting. Services like Proton Mail and Tuta don't. Your email address is one of the primary keys brokers use to match profiles across databases.

**Use a VPN from a trusted provider.** Mullvad and Proton VPN prevent IP and location tracking. This won't stop brokers who collect from public records or loyalty programmes, but it removes one major data source.

**Freeze your credit at the major bureaus.** This blocks brokers from pulling new financial data and prevents fraudulent account openings. It takes minutes and costs nothing.

**Use zero-knowledge encrypted services.** If your files, messages, and data are encrypted before they leave your device, there's nothing for brokers to collect — and nothing for hackers to steal in a breach. ## The Bottom Line

Data brokers have built a $312.84 billion industry on collecting, profiling, and trading your personal information without meaningful consent. They maintain thousands of data points on billions of people, suffer massive breaches, and make deletion deliberately difficult. The model depends on most people never noticing and never acting.

But you can act. Under GDPR, Europeans have enforceable deletion rights that authorities are increasingly willing to back with significant fines. Automated deletion services handle the tedious work of contacting hundreds of brokers. And zero-knowledge encryption removes the raw material brokers depend on entirely.

The data broker industry won't regulate itself. Oracle leaving the market and the FTC cracking down on Gravy Analytics are signs of change — but change is slow. Don't wait for it. Delete what you can, encrypt what you keep, and stop giving away what you don't have to.
