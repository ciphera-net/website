In 2019, Mark Zuckerberg published a [3,000-word manifesto](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/03/vision-for-social-networking/) calling encryption "the most important tool" for online privacy. He promised end-to-end encryption across all Meta messaging — Messenger, WhatsApp, and Instagram. Seven years later, Meta is doing the opposite. On [March 13, 2026](https://www.engadget.com/social-media/meta-is-killing-end-to-end-encryption-in-instagram-dms-195207421.html), the company announced it would remove E2EE from Instagram DMs entirely, effective May 8. That's [2 billion monthly active users](https://backlinko.com/instagram-users) losing encryption in one flip of a switch.

Meta's stated reason: "low adoption." But adoption was low by design. E2EE was opt-in, limited to select regions, and never promoted. The real question isn't why users didn't turn it on. It's why Meta never turned it on for them.

<BlogBlockquote variant="tldr">

Meta is removing end-to-end encryption from Instagram DMs on May 8, 2026, affecting 2 billion users ([Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/instagram-users), 2026). The move comes just 11 days before the Take It Down Act enforcement begins. Without E2EE, Meta can scan every Instagram message, comply with content requests, and feed conversation data into AI training. WhatsApp keeps its encryption. Instagram doesn't.

</BlogBlockquote>

## What Did Meta Actually Promise?

Zuckerberg's [March 2019 post](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/03/vision-for-social-networking/) was unambiguous. He wrote that "people increasingly want to connect privately" and that encryption is "the most important tool" to protect those conversations. Instagram with its [2 billion monthly users](https://backlinko.com/instagram-users) was explicitly included in that promise. The [EFF responded cautiously](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2019/03/privacy-focused-facebook-well-believe-it-when-we-see-it): "We'll believe it when we see it."

They were right to be sceptical. The timeline tells a damning story.

In 2022, Meta commissioned [BSR to conduct a human rights impact assessment](https://www.bsr.org/en/our-insights/report-view/metas-expansion-end-to-end-encryption) of its encryption plans. The finding was clear: encryption "supports a range of fundamental human rights" including privacy, physical safety, and freedom of expression. BSR made 45 recommendations. Meta committed to 34 of them.

For a deeper look at how encryption works in practice, see our [zero-knowledge encryption guide](/blog/zero-knowledge-encryption-guide).

Then Messenger got [default E2EE in December 2023](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/12/default-end-to-end-encryption-on-messenger/), built on the Signal Protocol combined with Meta's own Labyrinth Protocol. That part of the promise was kept. But Instagram? It got opt-in E2EE in "select regions" starting 2021. Never default. Never promoted. Never expanded beyond a test.

The arc is now complete: privacy manifesto (2019), partial opt-in testing (2021), removal announcement (March 13, 2026), E2EE ends (May 8, 2026). A seven-year arc from bold promise to quiet reversal.

## Why Is the Timing Suspicious?

Instagram E2EE ends May 8, 2026. The Take It Down Act enforcement begins May 19, 2026. Eleven days apart. That law requires platforms to remove non-consensual intimate imagery within 48 hours — and compliance is far easier when you can scan message content. Government data requests to Meta surged 675% from 2014 to 2024 ([Proton](https://proton.me/blog/big-tech-data-requests-surge), 2025).

Coincidence? Maybe. But the practical effect is undeniable.

Without E2EE, Meta regains the technical ability to scan every Instagram DM — text, photos, videos, voice messages. The Take It Down Act doesn't explicitly require removing encryption, but it does require content takedowns within tight deadlines. Scanning encrypted messages for compliance is, by definition, impossible. Removing encryption solves that problem neatly.

Then there's the advertising angle. Meta's December 2025 privacy policy update confirmed that interactions with Meta AI in private conversations can be used for ad targeting — affecting 1 billion monthly Meta AI users ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/01/meta-plans-to-sell-targeted-ads-based-on-data-in-your-ai-chats/), 2025). Unencrypted DMs become another data source in that pipeline.

Matthew Green, a Johns Hopkins cryptographer, compared Meta's "low adoption" excuse to "removing smoke detectors because they weren't frequently used." The analogy holds. You don't measure the value of safety infrastructure by how often people actively think about it.

For more on how surveillance trends are accelerating, see our [25 privacy statistics that define 2026](/blog/privacy-statistics-2026).

The surveillance numbers are stark. Meta received 322,062 government requests for user data in the second half of 2024 alone ([Meta Transparency](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/government-data-requests/), 2024). In the US, non-disclosure orders prevented Meta from notifying users about those requests 76.6% of the time ([Meta Transparency](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/government-data-requests/country/US/), H2 2024). FISA content requests increased 2,171% over the past decade ([Proton](https://proton.me/blog/big-tech-data-requests-surge), 2025).

When a platform can read your messages, so can anyone with a court order — or, increasingly, without one.

## The Child Safety Argument Is Real — But It's Not the Whole Story

NCMEC reports dropped 19% after Messenger got default E2EE — 6.9 million fewer reports, the largest single-year decline in NCMEC history ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/security/child-exploitation-watchdog-says-meta-encryption-led-sharp-decrease-ti-rcna205548), 2025). Meta accounts for over 67% of all NCMEC CyberTipline reports. This is the strongest counter-argument to encryption, and it shouldn't be dismissed.

But here's what the child safety framing leaves out.

The threat itself is shifting. AI-generated CSAM reports surged 1,325% in 2024 — from 4,700 to 67,000 reports ([NCMEC](https://www.missingkids.org/blog/2025/ncmec-releases-new-data-2024-in-numbers), 2025). This flood of synthetic material won't be caught by scanning real users' private messages. It's a different problem requiring different tools.

Client-side detection technology exists. Apple explored on-device scanning for CSAM, then abandoned the approach under privacy pressure. The technical capability to detect harmful content without reading every message is real — it just requires investment and careful implementation rather than the blunt instrument of removing encryption entirely.

**Our take:** Removing encryption for 2 billion users to catch bad actors is collective punishment. We don't open every postal letter to check for contraband. We don't wiretap every phone call to prevent crime. The EFF called this move "a capitulation that endangers journalists, dissidents, abuse survivors, and ordinary people." The child safety problem is genuine. The solution Meta chose protects nobody.

</BlogBlockquote>

## The Two-Tier Privacy Problem

WhatsApp keeps E2EE — 3 billion users protected by the Signal Protocol since 2016. Instagram loses it. Same company, opposite decisions. The 76% of Americans who say encryption is "very important" or "somewhat important" when choosing apps ([Proton](https://proton.me/learn/encryption/survey/us), June 2025) now face a split reality depending on which Meta app they use.

Why the difference? Geography and regulation tell most of the story.

WhatsApp dominates in the EU, where strict data protection law makes removing encryption politically and legally toxic. Instagram's user base skews younger and is concentrated in markets with weaker privacy frameworks. The calculus is straightforward: remove encryption where you can, keep it where you must.

The result is a two-tier system. Meta's more privacy-aware users on WhatsApp — who tend to be older, more international, and more vocal about data rights — get protection. Instagram's younger, more vulnerable demographic does not. And 41% of Americans incorrectly believe Gmail offers E2EE ([Proton](https://proton.me/learn/encryption/survey/us), June 2025), which suggests most people won't even notice what they've lost.

<BlogBlockquote>

**Our take:** Privacy is becoming a class divide. Those who know what to demand get encryption. Those who don't — disproportionately younger users, people in countries with weaker regulation, people who simply trust the apps they use — lose it. Meta didn't remove encryption from the product used by EU regulators and privacy advocates. It removed it from the one used by teenagers.

</BlogBlockquote>

## What Happens After May 8?

After May 8, 2026, every Instagram DM is readable by Meta. Text, photos, videos, voice messages — all of it transmitted and stored without end-to-end encryption. US non-disclosure orders prevent Meta from telling users when their data is handed to law enforcement 76.6% of the time ([Meta Transparency](https://transparency.meta.com/reports/government-data-requests/country/US/), H2 2024). You won't know. You can't know.

Meta can now run automated scanning on message content, comply with law enforcement content requests without technical barriers, and feed conversation data into AI training and ad targeting systems. The infrastructure for all of this already exists. Encryption was the only thing standing between those systems and your private conversations.

Meredith Whittaker, president of Signal, put it simply: "Companies that hold your data will eventually hand it over."
